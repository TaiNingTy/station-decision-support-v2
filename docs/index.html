AI product manager · portfolio case · public demonstration on open data

Turning transit-planning judgment into an AI workflow that shows its evidence

I led an AI decision-support product for configuring stations of a personal rapid transit (PRT) system. This page tells that story in STAR form, walks through what the product does on real public data for Miami's 21 Metromover stations, and is explicit about what is observed, what is derived, what is assumed, and what is not done.

The story

Situation, task, action, result

What actually happened, what I owned, and what can be verified. Claims about the real project are kept to what I did; the public demonstration extends the method on open data and is labelled as such.

S

Situation

At a PRT company, station-configuration knowledge lived with a few senior designers and in documents written for three teams (operations, engineering, urban design), each with its own standards. Proposals iterated slowly and quality depended on who was available. On a corridor study I worked on, raw activity heat had to be screened for service relevance, demand was dispersed, and the site's depth was constrained, which pushed the answer toward a smaller main station with distributed feeder stops that planning, engineering, operations and leadership all had to review.

T

Task

Own the product: turn that expertise into a grounded, auditable AI workflow that drafts a station configuration, cites its basis, and knows which decisions must stay human. Then, for V2, prove the approach on real public data with traceable provenance instead of synthetic site briefs, so that every number an interviewer clicks can be followed back to its source.

A

Action

  • Designed a fixed three-agent pipeline (requirement analysis → knowledge-grounded rule evaluation → configuration and delivery document) with a human-review handoff, choosing controllability and error localization over autonomy.
  • Diagnosed a retrieval hallucination (fluent citations of national standards that were not in the knowledge base) and fixed it: forced retrieval on every turn, knowledge-base-only citation, and an explicit "not covered by knowledge base" answer.
  • Moved hard engineering constraints out of the model into a deterministic code gate. A 27 m platform curve is BLOCKED and the generator never runs (0 tokens); 35 m passes and it runs.
  • For V2, rebuilt the data foundation on public sources for all 21 stations: station entities reconciled from 43 GTFS stops, zoning bands, ACS demography with margins of error, LODES jobs and job-to-home links, the GTFS timetable, published boardings and facilities, each value tagged observed / derived / assumption with an evidence id.
  • Wrote the demand-to-berth chain under PRT semantics (person trips → vehicle trips → berth cycles → platform module) with every parameter's provenance, and readiness flags that separate "complete" from "all observed".
  • Built a regression harness that rebuilds the whole pipeline in isolated copies and injects faults: corrupt packages, changed inputs, an optional layer that appears later, a publish that fails halfway.
R

Result

A reproducible pipeline for 21 stations whose rebuilds are byte-identical; a scenario chain that reports its own scale against observed boardings instead of pretending to be a forecast; PRT sizing per station for three scenarios; and an automated check suite that runs before anything is called done.

Deliberately not claimed: no calibration against counts, no validated walk catchment, no engineering design, and the V2 AI interpretation has not been run yet. The readiness view below says exactly that.

What I bring

Six habits this project shows

Each one is tied to something you can inspect in the demo or the repository, not to a slogan.

Framing the AI / human boundary

Hard where safety demands, soft where reasoning helps. Engineering constraints run as code; the model explains verdicts and prepares decisions that stay with people (feasibility, budget, final configuration).

Evidence: the V1.1 gate (0 tokens on a Critical FAIL) and the human-review handoff at the top of every delivery document.

Knowledge and rule governance

Knowledge-base-only citation, versioned rules, and "not covered" as a first-class answer. For a retrieval product the metric is not "can it answer" but "will it admit when it doesn't know".

Evidence: the documented hallucination diagnosis and fix; the AI usage rules carried inside every station input package.

Data semantics discipline

A five-year survey estimate is not current population; a job-to-home link is not a trip; a timetable is not ridership; a median is never averaged; an observed count is a reference, not a calibration target.

Evidence: every value in the demo carries a provenance class, period and uncertainty; the rules are written down before the code.

Evaluation mindset

Nothing is called verified until it has run. Runnable gate evals, a fault-injection harness for the data pipeline, and byte-identical rebuilds as the definition of "reproducible".

Evidence: the regression results linked at the bottom of this page.

Cross-functional alignment

Decision ownership is explicit: which numbers the owner must confirm (berth counts read from drawings, three renamed stations), which are assumptions (site space, vehicle occupancy), and who signs off engineering feasibility.

Evidence: the "assumption-backed" list in the readiness flags and the confirm-with-owner markers in the packages.

Honesty over polish

Status labels on this page are exported from the pipeline's readiness file, not typed by hand. Where a layer is missing, the page says NOT RUN instead of hiding the row.

Evidence: the ledger below, and the readiness step in the demo.

"I separate the need for a trip, the likelihood of choosing our service, and the policy governing access. Land use helps me interpret activity patterns, while income can inform affordability and price-sensitivity scenarios. Neither should automatically eliminate passenger demand. I translate those distinctions into explicit model assumptions, service requirements, and decision ownership, then make the trade-offs visible to planning, engineering, operations, and leadership."

How I think about demand, in one paragraph. The V2 rules cancelled two earlier defaults ("high income = willingness to pay" and "industrial zoning = no customers") for exactly this reason.

Working rules

Rules I wrote down before writing code

They are the reason the demo can be honest under questioning.

A five-year period estimate is reported with its period, vintage and 90 % margin of error, never as "current population".

Job counts and job-to-home links describe where people live and work; they are never called trips or ridership.

Per-station bands overlap. Station profiles are non-exclusive; the network total is computed once on the union geometry.

Medians are never averaged. Income brackets are aggregated and interpolated, and the open top bracket reports only a lower bound.

A density or accessibility map is not a persons-per-hour heat map. Peak boardings need time of day, purpose, mode choice, adoption and allocation, each tagged.

Observed boardings are a reference for scale and shape, never a calibration target, until origin-destination and hourly data exist.

PRT sizing uses average party size, not vehicle capacity, and a within-hour peaking factor that applies to berths only, never back to demand.

Precomputed does not mean no AI. The AI layer runs in preparation, keeps its run records, and cites evidence ids; the page never fakes a live call.
